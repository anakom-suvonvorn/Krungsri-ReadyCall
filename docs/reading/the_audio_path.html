Where the Sentence Goes

Three things landed this session: your idea about the queue ceiling, the GPU finally doing real work, and the layer that turns a caller's voice into words on the agent's screen. This is all of it in plain language — and the last section is nothing but commands you can run to check I am telling the truth.

Nothing here needs you to read any code. Every claim on this page is either something you can see in a terminal, or is labelled as a thing I have not verified.

Part one — your idea

The wait ceiling now depends on how urgent you are

Some background, because the rest only makes sense with it. The system does not answer calls in the order they arrive. Every second it looks at everyone waiting and every free agent at once, and works out the best overall pairing. Two things decide it: how well an agent fits that caller, and how urgent the caller is. Urgency grows the longer you wait.

That is good — but on its own it means an unlucky caller could wait forever if everyone else keeps fitting better. So there has always been a backstop: past a certain wait, stop optimising and just connect them to anyone qualified.

Until this session that backstop was one number for everybody: 180 seconds. That is what you spotted a problem with, and you were right.

The problem, concretely

One agent free. Two people want them. A routine policy question that has been holding for 181 seconds, and someone at a crash scene who has been holding for ten.

The routine caller is past the single 180-second ceiling. The emergency is not. So the routine caller gets rescued — handed the agent, no argument — and the emergency waits. The emergency has to sit there for another 170 seconds before the system will treat them as urgent by that rule. That is obviously the wrong way round, and it is exactly what you proposed fixing.

What shipped

Each urgency level now has its own ceiling. These live in a config file, so changing them is editing a text file — no code, no rebuild:

UrgencyCeilingMeaning, read as a promise
critical60sat a crash scene, a medical emergency
high120s
normal180sunchanged — this was the old single value
low270snot in any hurry

Two details you did not ask about, but should know

If two people are both past their own ceiling, the more urgent goes first. That was your instinct and I implemented it. Within the same tier, the longest wait wins.

The table cuts both ways, and this half is easy to miss. A low caller at 200 seconds is past the old 180s ceiling but not their own 270s one. So they are no longer yanked out of the careful matching and handed whoever is nearest — they stay where fit still counts. Being "rescued" deliberately gives you a worse-matched agent (more on that below), so not doing it to someone who is not actually suffering is a real improvement.

The one cost, stated plainly. If emergencies keep arriving without pause, a patient caller past their ceiling can be overtaken again and again. It needs a genuine flood of emergencies to bite. There is an alternative ordering that self-balances (rank by how far past your own ceiling you are, as a ratio) — I did not use it because the system already treats "critical" as a category rather than a big number, and "emergencies first" is something you can explain to a supervisor. It is written down as the fix if this ever shows up in practice.

Why a rescued caller gets the worst qualified agent

This surprises people, so: everyone in that pool has already been checked as able to handle the call. Fit only says how well. If a starved caller took the best specialist, you would fix one person's wait by starting someone else's — the person who actually needed that specialist. The promise is somebody competent, right now, and spending the scarcest agent on it is the opposite of what the backstop is for.

Guard rail: a table where a more urgent tier waits longer than a less urgent one now refuses to start the system at all. Getting it backwards would otherwise be completely silent — every call still routes, and only the crash-scene caller quietly waits.
Part two — the GPU

The libraries are installed, and they are the right ones

You asked for the CUDA build rather than the CPU one. That was the right thing to insist on, and it is worth knowing why it matters so much here.

Installing the wrong one does not fail. There is no error. Everything imports, everything runs, tests pass — and the speech model runs roughly ten times too slowly on the processor instead of the graphics card, while the flag that says "am I using the GPU?" quietly reads False. You would find out on stage.

So the project now pins that one package to the CUDA download source, and the check is written into the setup guide. What actually got installed and verified:

Part three — the main build

Turning a voice into words on the agent's screen

Here is the gap this filled. Since last session, a caller could press 1 to record a message while they wait, and there was a method sitting there ready to receive their sentences — with nothing on the other end of it. The plumbing from "microphone" to "that method" did not exist. Now it does.

Five stages. Follow one sentence through them:

Stage 1 — make everything look the same

A phone line does not deliver audio in the form a speech model wants. It arrives at half the sample rate, compressed with one of two different schemes depending on the country, and sometimes in two channels. One module converts all of it, once, at the edge, so nothing further in ever has to ask what it got.

Thailand uses the compression scheme called A-law; North America and Japan use µ-law. Decoding one as the other does not produce an error — it produces loud, distorted, entirely believable audio, and the first thing anyone blames is the microphone or the model. Both are supported, and the format is stated rather than guessed. You can see the difference for yourself in check 4 below.

Stage 2 and 3 — find where sentences begin and end

A detector answers one narrow question thirty times a second: is anyone speaking right now? It returns a probability, not a yes/no. Turning that into whole sentences is a separate job with its own rules — and those rules came from your earlier Thai project, not from guesswork:

  • Speech must last at least half a second to count. Below that it is a cough or a door.
  • A pause under a tenth of a second does not end a sentence, because Thai speakers pause mid-clause.
  • Each sentence is extended 120 milliseconds backwards — this one matters most. Without it the model clips the first syllable, and in Thai the first syllable often carries the tone that decides which word it is.

All three of those live in one small file with no model and no audio in it, which means they can be tested by feeding in a list of numbers. That is why they cannot quietly drift into something that merely sounds fine on one recording.

Stages 4 and 5 — transcribe, then check

Finished sentences go into a queue, and one worker transcribes them one at a time. Two things fall out of that, both deliberate:

  • Audio keeps arriving while the model is busy. If the pipeline waited for the model, the caller would still be talking with nowhere for their words to go.
  • Sentences stay in the order they were spoken. The obvious alternative — transcribe everything at once — finishes a two-word phrase before the long sentence that came first, and delivers the caller's words shuffled.
Part four — the interesting part

The measurement that nearly fooled me

This is the part worth your attention, because it changed the design and because it is a good story about not trusting your own tools.

I built a harness to compare speech engines — the thing that is supposed to answer "which model should we use?" with numbers instead of opinions. I ran it on the real graphics card. It gave me numbers. I did not believe them, and I was right not to.

Three of the numbers were the tool being broken, not the system

It reportedWhat was actually wrong
0 ms delay, for every engine The stopwatch never started. It looked for the end of each sentence at an exact position, and sentences end 60 ms later than that because of the backwards-padding rule. It missed every single time, recorded nothing, and printed a clean zero.
0 MB of graphics memory It asked the wrong component. The speech engine manages its own memory, so the counter I used could not see a single byte of it.
11,270 ms delay I was feeding a 4.5-second file in as fast as the disk could read it, so all three sentences arrived at once and the third waited behind two others. That is a backlog, not a delay. Paced at real speed, the same work: 553 ms.
A clean zero is the giveaway. A real measurement of something fast jitters; an exact 0.0 across unrelated things means the instrument, not the code. This project has hit that before — the very same lesson is written up from a previous session.

And then the real finding

With honest instruments, I measured the model directly on three inputs:

InputTimeWhat came back
0.9s of speech-like sound155 msnothing — correct, it was not speech
2.0s of speech-like sound195 msnothing — correct
1.0s of pure silence8,578 ms"ช่วงที่สุดได้โลงในอ้าอีก นี่" — invented

Silence costs 55 times what real speech costs, and comes back with words nobody said. The invention was a known quirk and was written down. The time was not. That changes what it means: if the detector wrongly thinks it heard something, that is not one junk line on a screen — it is an eight-second blockage in front of every real sentence behind it.

And it gets worse. To help the model with insurance words, we hand it a short list of terms — กรมธรรม์ (policy), สินไหม (claim), and so on. Fed silence with that list, it returned "สินไหม? กรมธรรม์, ผู้เอาประกัน, ผู้เอาประกัน" — three of our own words, in our own file's order, as if the caller had said them.

That is worse than ordinary nonsense. Those are precisely the words that make a summary look credible, and an agent reading them has no way to know they were invented.

The three guards

  1. Check the sound before the model sees it. If a "sentence" has no actual energy in it, it never reaches the model. Two multiplications, and it removes the eight-second problem entirely.
  2. Refuse text that is mostly our own word list. Carefully: a real sentence that merely contains "claim" must survive, or the guard deletes exactly the calls it was added to help. It triggers on proportion, not presence.
  3. Refuse repetition loops. This one existed already but was too easily fooled — the real output had a comma after every word, which hid the repetition from it.
My own code had the same bug I was hunting. Both engines "warmed up" by transcribing one second of silence — the single most expensive input the model has. Fixed to use a tone.

Part six — what I did not do

Three things are deliberately unfinished

1. There is no engine comparison table yet, and that is on purpose

The harness that produces it is built and correct. What it needs is real Thai telephone speech, plus the Thai model weights — another multi-gigabyte download I left for you to approve, exactly like the libraries.

I could have filled that table using the synthetic test tones. I did not, because it would have been meaningless: as part four shows, what the model does with tones tells you about its failure modes, not its accuracy. A table of confident, wrong numbers is worse than an empty one.

To finish it: put a real recording and a text file of what was actually said side by side (call.wav and call.txt) and the accuracy column fills itself in. A phone recording beats a clean studio one — the whole point is to measure the conditions we will actually have.

2. Nothing saves the audio

The plan says the recording should be encrypted and stored. It is not, and nothing is written to disk at all right now — everything happens in memory and is discarded. That needs the encryption-key work from a later phase, and writing a caller's voice to disk before that exists would be the one thing the design was careful to avoid.

3. The agent cannot see the transcript live yet

The sentences reach the system and are published internally. Nothing draws them on the workstation screen. That is a front-end job and it is next door, not far away.

Added 2 September, evening — after your comments

Four things you raised, and what each one turned up

Read this before the sections above. Three of your four comments found a real bug, and two of them are in code described earlier on this page as working.

1. Your loop examples broke the guard completely

You pasted three transcriptions you had actually seen from Thonburian — "คนเชื่อถือในการการการการ…" and two like it. I tested the guard described in part four against them. 

users real #1   caught = False
users real #2   caught = False
users real #3   caught = False

It caught none of them. The guard split the text on spaces — and Thai does not put spaces between words, so all three are a single 200-character token and it gave up before looking at anything. It only ever worked on the one hallucination I happened to see, which came back with commas in it.

It is character-level now: it finds the longest repeating chunk and calls it a loop when that chunk buries most of the sentence. Catches 3 of 3. Four "must survive" cases guard the other direction, because Thai genuinely repeats syllables (เร็วๆ, ค่อยๆ) and a guard that ate real sentences would be worse than no guard.

2. Your timing idea — adopted, in a stronger form

Your instinct was to use a signal from a different axis than the text patterns. That is the valuable part and it is right. But decode time needs a baseline per model and per graphics card, which we would barely have on demo day — and time is really just a proxy for how much text came out.

So: characters per second of speech. Same idea, no calibration, no clock. And your dataset let me measure the threshold instead of guessing it:

What it does not do: it detects, it cannot prevent. By the time you know the output was impossible, the eight seconds are spent. Actually stopping a runaway needs the model in a separate process that can be killed — which the architecture already plans — so I wrote that down rather than faking it with a timeout that does not work.

3. You were right about the models

faster_whisper_tiny was a 75 MB check that the graphics card worked at all, not a candidate. Also: Typhoon ASR is not a Whisper model at all. It is a different architecture entirely (NVIDIA NeMo), which is exactly why it is worth measuring — it does not pad everything to 30 seconds, it genuinely streams, and it has no free-running text generator to hallucinate with. Added 3 September — the answer to the open question above

The bad score was our bug, not the model's

The last time I wrote this page it ended with an honest admission: the speech model was scoring 0.47 to 0.76 on real Thai calls, where 0 is perfect and 1 is completely wrong. That is bad. I listed four possible reasons and said none of them had been ruled out.

None of the four was the answer. The real one was a bug in our own code, and the score is now 0.09 to 0.50, typically 0.29 — the same model, the same audio, the same day.

How the four were eliminated, cheapest first

This ordering is the part worth keeping. Each check was chosen to be the fastest way to disprove something, not to confirm it.

The theoryThe checkVerdict
We transcribe far more audio than the human-written answer key covers, so every extra word counts against us. The key covers only 15–29% of each recording. So: how loud is the other 75%? Measured second by second. Dead. It is digital silence — this is one side of a phone call, quiet while the other person talks. There were no extra words available to be wrong.
The part that decides "someone is speaking now" is throwing sentences away. Wrote a scorer for it — the first thing ever to read the dataset's own annotations. It said 20% of speech never reached the model. Looked guilty, and was not. Measuring the loudness of those seconds rather than counting them: 86% are near-silent and 72% sit within half a second of where a human drew the boundary. That is a person rounding a mark outward, not a lost sentence. Real speech lost: 3%.
A vocabulary hint is not being passed to the model. Already known and already written down. Real, but small. Not capable of explaining a score that bad.
Real phone audio is just harder than what the model was advertised on. Deliberately last — it is the one theory that cannot be acted on, which makes it the most comfortable and the least useful. Never needed.

What found it was looking at the words

There was no way to see the actual text behind the score. So I added one — and the answer was immediate. The model's output was fluent, correct Thai that matched the end of the answer key, with the beginning simply missing. 48 characters where there should have been 112, and all 48 right.

Transcribing each piece of the call one at a time produced all six, every one correct. So the model was fine. The detector was fine. The safety guards were fine. The losses were happening in the plumbing between them.

What the plumbing was doing. Audio is held in a buffer while the model works through a queue. The buffer was throwing away anything older than 30 seconds behind the newest piece of audio that came in — which is correct only when the model keeps up. When it fell behind, the buffer discarded the caller's opening sentences before the model was ever shown them.
And the half that is genuinely dangerous. When the audio for a piece was gone, the code did not report that. It quietly handed back a different stretch of the call of about the right length. In Thai, that transcribes into a perfectly fluent sentence — attributed to a moment when the caller was saying something else entirely. It would have reached an agent's screen looking exactly as trustworthy as a real one. A missing sentence is a gap you can see; a confident wrong one is not.

What changed

  • Every piece of audio waiting in the queue now holds a claim on the buffer, and nothing older than the oldest claim can be released.
  • If the model falls more than two minutes behind, the oldest piece is given up with a warning in the log. It cannot be given up silently, and the queue cannot grow until the program runs out of memory.
  • If a piece's audio is gone anyway, it is refused. It is never approximated from whatever happens to be left.
  • Both places that used to discard something without a word now say so.
The tests were checked against the broken version first. A test that passes on code you already fixed proves nothing. On the old code these fail exactly as they should — three sentences go in, one comes out. And they assert something stronger than "three results arrived": the fake model reports the loudness of the audio it was handed, so the test can insist that result three carried segment three's audio. That stronger assertion is the only kind that could have caught the dangerous half.

The real problem was underneath it all along

There is a flag on the measuring tool called --fast. It feeds the audio in as quickly as possible instead of at the speed a real person talks. I added it earlier because the tool had been lying about speed — and then used it for every measurement afterwards, which is what let the bug above survive.

Running it properly, at real speaking speed, shows the thing that actually matters:

TargetMeasured
Time from a caller finishing a sentence to it appearing on screen under 1.5 s 4.4 – 7.7 s

This is now the open problem, and it is a much better one to have than a mysterious accuracy score. The model takes about three seconds per sentence on this laptop's graphics card, and sentences are handled one at a time, so three short phrases in quick succession queue up behind each other. There are known things to try — a compressed build of the same model, and a different model that does not pad every clip to 30 seconds the way this family does. Both were always planned; now there is a number saying which question they answer.

Worth being clear about the stakes: nothing here delays the actual phone call. The caller is connected to a human on a path that never waits for any of this. What a slow transcript costs is how much of the caller's own words the agent has in front of them at the moment they pick up.

If something here does not match what you see, that is worth telling me — two of the four problems in part four were found by exactly that, looking at a number and saying "that cannot be right".
