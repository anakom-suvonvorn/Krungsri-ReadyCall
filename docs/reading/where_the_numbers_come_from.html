D123 · the app

Asking about cover you do not have

In the app, Contact us — something else used to go straight to a list of admin things: change my details, request a document, pay a premium, complain. So a customer could ask about a policy they hold, and about their own paperwork, and could not ask about buying anything.

The options were not missing. They were sitting in the per-line reason menus, in the general context, reachable from the phone keypad and from nowhere else. ซื้อประกันเดินทาง — buy travel insurance — is the clean example: D122 correctly filtered it out of the "about this plan" list, and it had no other home.

On screen

Tapping something else now asks which kind of cover first — motor, health, travel, life, or เรื่องอื่นๆ — and then shows that line's reasons.

Underneath

GET /v1/app/contact-lines returns the keypad's own first menu, straight out of menus.yaml. Picking one calls the existing contact-reasons endpoint with context=general.

The rule

That list is deliberately not filtered. contexts describes reasons; this question is what establishes the context. A customer who has not tapped a policy has told us exactly as little as somebody who has just dialled.

Two details worth knowing. unknown is a row in that list rather than a special case — it is the keypad's own เรื่องอื่นๆ, and following it lands on the admin menu, exactly where this branch used to start. Nothing was taken away; a step was put in front. And no config changed: one read-only endpoint over the menu the app was already reading.

Found on the way: motor.advice.quote, health.advice.quote and life.advice.quote exist as intents, with slots and playbooks, and no menu reaches any of them — only travel's does. Recorded as Q36. Splitting "buy" from "compare" would add a seventh option to three phone menus, which is a domain call rather than an engineering one.

D124 · the call

Handing the call to the insurer

Since D117 a claim call arrives with a banner saying it ends with the insurer, because adjudicating and paying claims is their duty and not a broker's. The banner was the entire feature. The broker read it, said the sentence, pressed วางสาย, and typed whatever they remembered into the wrap-up. Which company, and why, existed nowhere.

On screen

ส่งต่อ sits beside วางสาย. The dialog offers the carrier that wrote this customer's policy as its own choice above a menu of the market, then a closed list of reasons.

Underneath

The handoff is recorded, then the call is ended — in that order. The wrap-up form renders the instant the state changes, so a prefill computed afterwards is one nobody ever sees.

The rule

The record beats the menu. config/insurers.yaml is a list somebody typed; Policy.insurer is a fact about this customer. The policy's carrier is offerable whether or not it appears in the file.

That last rule is the one to keep. On hackathon day the real data arrives carrying carriers nobody has typed into config — and a broker who cannot hand a claim to the company that actually wrote the policy has no product at all. A startup check demanding the two agree would refuse to boot on exactly the data the whole CoreDataProvider seam exists to absorb.

The request carries a reason code and either an insurer code or a flag meaning "the carrier on their policy". There is no field for a company name, so a client cannot file a handoff to a company that never wrote anything for this customer — and the wrap-up would have recorded that as fact.

Why it does not use CallState.TRANSFERRED

The obvious move, and wrong twice. That state is terminal, so a call in it could never reach WRAP_UP — and after-call work on a handoff is real work: what was gathered, what the insurer will need, what the customer was told. Making it non-terminal instead would leave two states that both mean "the media is over and the agent is filing", and every reader of "is this agent in after-call work" would have to check both. That is how B25 and B26 happened.

So a handoff ends the call the ordinary way, and what makes it a handoff is the record: a handed_to_insurer:<code> transition reason, a call.handed_off event, and a disposition the broker confirms. A test asserts TRANSFERRED stays unentered, so nobody tidies it back without reading why.

Nothing is written until the broker presses save. The handoff prefills the wrap-up as a suggestion; the durable statement is the call_wrapups row they submit, because they are the one who made the promise to the customer.

D125 · the data

Why the plan list is not a config file

Every earlier note said to put the comparison data in config/products.yaml, beside the intents and the menus. The question that stopped it was yours: "shouldn't the comparison data come from the company's database, not a yaml?"

It should, and the reasoning generalises into a test you can apply to anything:

If the answer changes because…Then it is…And it lives…
an insurer launched or withdrew a plan data owned by somebody else behind CoreDataProvider
we decided a room rate matters more than an outpatient limit config — our own knowledge config/comparison.yaml

The practical half is sharper than the philosophical one. config/ is not reachable by the hackathon-day data swap. The entire point of the port is that "the data is not what we assumed" becomes a config line and a green contract suite rather than a rewrite — so putting the one thing the comparison reads outside that seam would have meant the feature the pitch leans on hardest was the single feature the swap could not fix.

What landed: list_products(line=…, active_only=…) on the port and on every adapter, Product with an insurer and typed coverage rows — the same rows a policy uses, read by one parser, because a gap analysis compares what the customer holds against what a plan offers and two shapes for one concept is two mapping bugs.

There is no premium field, and that is the decision rather than an omission. Pricing and underwriting are out of scope. A plan says what it covers; what it costs this customer depends on their age, their risk and the insurer's own underwriting. Surface and compare — never quote.

Writing the contract test found two things that were quietly broken. EMP-GROUP-HEALTH and KS-MOTOR-2ND were on policies and in no catalogue at all, so looking up the plan behind two of the demo customer's three policies returned nothing — and the only symptom would have been an empty column on somebody's phone. And the same carrier was spelled two ways: a policy said อลิอันซ์ อยุธยา while the catalogue said อลิอันซ์ อยุธยา ประกันภัย, and that suffix is what separates the general company from the life company.

D126 · the logic

The ranking, worked out by hand

This is the part worth reading slowly, because it is the answer to "is the AI picking the plan?" — and the answer is no, and that is deliberate.

Every position in the table is arithmetic. A model, when one is wired in, rewrites only the reason sentence on top of an ordering it did not choose. A model that ranks is a model that can be argued into putting a plan first; a model that explains an arithmetic ranking cannot.

Here is the actual sum, on the real demo customer. Their employer group plan caps a hospital room at ฿1,500 a day, which is the gap the whole health story turns on.

Read the Bangkok Elite column. It has the best figure in three of the five rows and it still ranks below Muang Thai, because a ฿30,000 excess costs it −1.00 at weight 2. That is the ranking doing real work rather than sorting by the biggest number — and it is the moment worth showing a judge.

Four arithmetic rules make that honest, and each one was a specific way to lie to somebody choosing insurance:

  1. Absent is not zero. A plan silent on outpatient cover and a plan that excludes it are different products. A missing figure moves the score in neither direction, renders as "—", and can never win its row. That is why the copay row above decides nothing: the customer's own plan does not state one, so there is nothing to compare against.
  2. Improvement is clipped to +2.00 / −1.00. Bangkok Elite's annual limit is fifty times what they hold; without the clip that one number would decide the table, and a comparison whose order is set by a single outlier is a sales script with a table around it.
  3. Below 5%, "better" is not reported as better. A 2% higher room rate is not a reason to change carrier. The figure still renders — the customer sees both numbers — it is simply not called an improvement.
  4. The plan they already hold is never a candidate. It is the column everything is measured against. Offering it back is a table recommending the status quo it was asked to challenge.

And a fifth, which only appeared once the output was looked at. With no policy to improve on, every weighted improvement is exactly zero — which is not a tie, it is no ranking at all, and the order then falls back to whatever the catalogue happened to be in. That is precisely how an affiliated carrier ends up silently first. New business ranks candidates against each other instead, one attribute at a time.

The reason sentence names what is worse as well as what is better, for the same reason the red chips on screen are exactly as large as the green ones: a panel that shows only the upside is a sales script.

D126 · the part that nearly shipped wrong

The column that was on the wrong screen

The table's first column is headed แผนปัจจุบันของคุณ and carries the customer's real coverage figures. The tool that pushes it is declared personal: false — so the first time it was pushed, all of that landed on a screen that had only tapped a link. Tapping a link proves somebody is holding that phone. It does not prove who they are.

Both obvious fixes are wrong. Marking the tool personal walls off the one part of this journey with no privacy cost at all — comparing what the market offers is true for anybody, and gating it is the ordinary and wrong design. Leaving it is a stranger's cover on whoever holds the handset.

An absence explains nothing, so the customer's page says เข้าสู่ระบบเพื่อเทียบกับแผนปัจจุบันของคุณเอง where the column would be — otherwise a guest concludes we do not hold their policy.

D127 · the screen

The plan panel, and the bug it exposed

Your note was that the comparison "is just there on the side on the right, put into there with not much thought at all" — which was fair. It was a five-column table living in a 340-pixel lane, permanently expanded, needing its own scrollbar to exist. The feature had arrived before a place for it did.

Your framing named the missing thing: a broker does not want "the comparison", they want what the market offers. Comparison is one answer to that. Reading a single plan's figures out loud is the same question at a different zoom.

On screen

A compact ข้อมูลแผนประกัน panel showing the current best match in one line, opening a dialog with a line selector and two tabs: เปรียบเทียบ and แผนทั้งหมด.

Underneath

GET …/plans?line= returns the catalogue for a line with every figure the ranking uses. Pushing one plan sends a product code; the server reads what it covers.

The rule

The rail is where you see there is something to look up; the dialog is where you look it up. The preview line keeps the useful half visible at zero width cost.

The line selector broke something the moment it existed

Switching the dialog to motor during a health call announced ลูกค้ายังไม่มีความคุ้มครองในหมวดนี้ — to a customer holding a motor policy — and then ranked the motor catalogue as new business. Not just a missing column: a different question, answered silently.

The baseline was relevant_policy, the policy this call is about. Right for the policy panel and for a handoff; wrong the instant the broker asks about another line — which is the entire point of a broker's portfolio. It now reads the whole portfolio and takes the policy on the line being asked about. Motor finds MT-2024-008830; a line they hold nothing on still compares, as new business.

Worth noticing that this is B30's lesson again — a fixture with one of something tests nothing about choosing — and that it only surfaced because D117 gave the demo customer cover from three carriers across two lines in the first place.

D128 · your idea

The one thing the broker writes

Every other control on the rail sends something the system composed from a record: a comparison, a prefilled form, a checklist. Your idea was the same box pointed the other way — the broker types, and the customer reads.

It is useful for exactly the thing the phone is bad at: a hospital name, a spelling, a reference number, three steps to follow. Line breaks survive, because an address has them.

This is the one tool where the tier gate cannot help

personal describes what a tool is for. This one is for whatever the broker needs to say — the system cannot classify a sentence it did not write. Marking it personal would wall off the ordinary case (a hospital's name is true for anybody) and would not stop the broker typing a policy number anyway.

So the composer puts the screen's tier in front of them while they type: ลูกค้ายังไม่ได้เข้าสู่ระบบ — เลี่ยงข้อมูลส่วนบุคคล, or green once they have signed in. Give the person the fact and let them decide, rather than fake a refusal the system is not equipped to make. They are already choosing what to say out loud to whoever holds that phone; this is the same judgement with the screen's status visible for once.

The customer is told a person wrote it — ข้อความจากเจ้าหน้าที่ sits above the text. Everything else on that screen is the system quoting a record, and a typed sentence rendered identically would borrow an authority the writer did not claim.

It also made an existing hazard real: /sim was dropping text_th into innerHTML unescaped. Harmless while every payload was server-composed; a script injection with a keyboard attached the moment a human types into the box. Both customer surfaces escape now.

Not built: images and files. You asked, and it is a scope call rather than a shrug. Agent-to-customer files need blob storage on the assist path, a size and type policy, and a retention rule — the same list Q34 already has open. The text half is the part that removes the failure being solved, and it needs none of that.

The thread

Who is allowed to compose what

Six changes, one question. Everything that reaches the customer's screen has an author, and the system is strict about which author is allowed for which kind of content.

What reaches the customerWho composes itBecause
A coverage figure, in a table or a plan detail the server, from the record A figure a browser assembled is a figure nobody can trace to a field
The ordering of a comparison the server, arithmetic from config weights A ranking that can be argued with is a ranking that can be bought
The reason sentence beside each plan a model, eventually — generated for now It may explain an ordering; it may never choose one
Which carrier a call is handed to the server, from a code the client picked A client that could send a company name could invent one
A sentence the broker types the broker, and it says so on screen The one case the system cannot classify, so the human decides — with the tier in front of them

The last row is the exception, and it is the one that proves the rule is about capability rather than trust: everywhere the system knows enough to compose the content, it does, and the client only ever names which. Where it does not know — a human's own sentence — it says who wrote it and shows the writer what kind of screen they are writing to.
