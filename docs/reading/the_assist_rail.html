Stop Describing the Screen

Four things landed in one day: the domain became a broker's instead of an insurer's, the agent's playbooks moved into config, the AI actually runs, and a phone call can now be bound to the customer's own phone so the broker pushes a form onto it instead of reading directions aloud.

The finding underneath all of it

A broker is not a small insurance company

Page 30 of the orientation deck is Krungsri's own slide, and it splits the work in two. Read the right column and the left column as two different jobs, because that is what they are.

Before this, the system had motor.claim and health.claim skills and a queue called IPD pre-authorisation. That is a single insurer's call centre wearing a broker's name — and a broker screen that implies it can approve an admission is doing underwriting, which the brief puts explicitly out of scope.

The whole change was config plus one field. No routing, matching, presence or transcript logic moved. That is worth saying in the pitch: retargeting this machine is a config swap, and here is the receipt.

The banner that appears before anyone speaks

On a claim call the workstation now shows, above the policy panel:

เรื่องนี้ต้องส่งต่อบริษัทประกัน — เราช่วยรวบรวมข้อมูลและประสานงานให้

Because “I'll check and call you back” and “I'm passing you to the insurer now” are different promises, and only one of them is a broker's to make.

And the one field that makes any of it possible

Every policy now records which company underwrote it. A single insurer's system has no use for that — every policy in it is theirs. A broker's whole record is one customer's cover spread across carriers, and which carrier decides who a claim goes to and whose terms a comparison is against.

The demo customer holds three policies from three different real carriers, including employer group health capped at ฿1,500/day — which is the gap the whole health story turns on.

Deliberate: the Krungsri-affiliated carrier is one row among several, and sometimes not the recommended one. A broker whose affiliate always wins is not a broker — and that is the version that survives a judge asking about it.

Your idea, built

Binding the call to the customer's phone

The problem you named at the orientation: a broker on the phone saying “go to the website, tap the menu at the top right, then Documents, then Upload.” The brief prices exactly this — step 4 of the journey leaks on เอกสารเยอะ ลูกค้า drop-off กลางทาง.

So stop describing the screen and start filling it. Once the call and a screen are bound, the broker pushes; the customer never navigates.

Why a link rather than a code

The call is already on a phone number, and caller ID tells us which. So a link sent to that number proves as much as the caller ID does, and costs one tap.

A code the customer reads back is genuinely stronger — it proves they are looking at the screen and on the call. It is also a chore handed to somebody who rang because they were already stuck. The system takes the link and lets the sign-in carry the rest.

The refusal is the feature, not an error

Push a form to a screen that has only tapped a link and the broker gets: “form needs a signed-in screen; ask the customer to sign in first.”

That message exists so the broker asks — instead of a stranger's policy details appearing on whoever happens to be holding that handset. It is the single most important line in the whole feature, and it is worth watching it fire rather than trusting that it does.

This is also the answer to “do we have to make them register?”

No — not to be helped. Somebody comparing plans gets everything they need without an account, because none of it is about them. The wall appears only where the content is personal. Putting registration first would gate the half that has no privacy cost at all, which is the ordinary and wrong design.

And the “agent help mode” button

You were right that an always-visible button is odd. But the better answer is that the customer never has to find one: the server knows a call is live for this signed-in person, so the app is told and offers. They do not go looking at the exact moment they are least able to.

That is also the first time identity resolution does something beyond “how sure are we” — it now also decides whether the app may say “you're on a call — want the agent to fill this in for you?”

The AI

Six ways the summary does nothing, and one where it helps

The model now genuinely runs. What matters more is what happens when it does not, because on a laptop with no key that is the only path.

The summary is started when the agent presses Accept and never waited for. The agent is connected the instant that button returns, already reading the rule-based summary — which quotes the caller word for word and cannot invent anything. If the model answers, the screen quietly upgrades. If it never answers, nothing happens at all.

The model stated a coverage figure — refused outright

That last row is the one to keep. Coverage amounts are read from the policy record, never written by a model. The prompt forbids stating figures and a guard refuses the output if one appears anyway — because the guard assumes the prompt will one day fail. An invented “คุ้มครองสูงสุด 3,000 บาทต่อวัน” is precisely the plausible, credible, wrong number that makes a brief dangerous.

Try it

Fifteen minutes, no key, no GPU

Start the system and sign in at /workstation as A006, then press พร้อมรับสาย.

uv run python -m readycall.entrypoints.api

Place a claim call — this is the one that shows the broker/insurer split:

curl -X POST http://127.0.0.1:8000/v1/demo/calls \
  -H "Content-Type: application/json" \
  -d '{"intent_code":"health.claim.notify","caller_number":"0812345678","intake_keys":["2"],"ignore_hours":true}'

Press Accept. You should see the carrier name under the policy number, the hand-over banner above it, and three recommended actions. Attest the identity and the deeper steps appear — those are the ones that involve acting on the policy.

Then the customer's screen

Get the pairing link for that call, open it in another window, and run the sequence in this order:

  1. Push a comparison. It lands on the other screen within a second, with no sign-in.
  2. Push a form. Refused — read the message.
  3. Sign in on the customer screen, push the form again. It appears, prefilled.
  4. Fill it in and submit. The broker's side shows exactly what was typed.

Step 2 is the one to actually do. Watching it refuse is far more convincing than reading that it would.

To see the real AI

Install the extra, switch the provider, and place a call where the caller actually speaks. Then watch the summary on screen replace itself a few seconds after you are already talking.

uv sync --extra web --extra llm    # name every extra you want — sync prunes the rest

And to prove the call is never blocked on AI rather than trusting it: point the provider at a port with nothing on it, and place a call. It connects exactly as fast, the rule-based summary stays, and one warning appears in the log.

Honest list

What is not built

  • The comparison data. The table renders and reaches the phone — but what fills it is still hand-written in the demo. Ranking real plans against what the customer already holds is the next track.
  • Sending the link. No SMS, no LINE yet. The link comes back for the broker to read out — which is also what a rehearsal needs.
  • Signature, OCR, document upload. The request is recorded; the rest is stubbed. One tool working properly beats five that half-work.
  • One customer app. The old simulator and the new assist screen are still two separate pages.
  • A labelled set for the AI. Without one, “85% intent accuracy” stays a claim rather than a measurement.
